SESSION 8 · NPV EXPLAINED
NPV: what it measures and how to run it
Why discount?
₹1 today can be invested to earn r, so ₹1 received in year t is worth 1/(1+r)^t today. The discount rate is the return on equally risky alternatives.
The 4 steps
1. Forecast incremental after-tax cash flows.
2. Pick r (project risk, usually WACC).
3. Discount each flow.
4. Add up and subtract the outlay.
NPV
Meaning
Decision
> 0
Earns more than r; adds value
Accept
= 0
Earns exactly r
Indifferent
< 0
Earns less than r
Reject
Common pitfalls
Use incremental cash flows only (ignore sunk costs); include working capital and tax; use cash, not accounting profit; match r to project risk.
4
NPV measures rupees of value and is additive across independent projects.

SESSION 8 · IRR EXPLAINED
IRR: the break-even rate, found by interpolation
Definition
IRR is the r that makes NPV = 0. Accept if IRR > cost of capital.
Interpolation
IRR ≈ r_low + NPV_low / (NPV_low − NPV_high) × (r_high − r_low)

SESSION 9 · CAPM IN DETAIL
The model, its logic and its assumptions
Formula
E(Ri) = Rf + βi × [E(Rm) − Rf]
Rf: reward for time. (Rm − Rf): market risk premium. β: how much market risk the stock carries.
Logic
Firm-specific risk (strike, recall, fraud) diversifies away for free, so it earns no premium. Market risk (rates, inflation, 2008, COVID-19) cannot, so CAPM pays for it.
Assumption
Reality check
Investors are rational, hold diversified portfolios
Behavioural biases exist
One-period horizon, same expectations
Views differ
Borrow and lend at Rf, no taxes or costs
Borrowing costs more than Rf
Market portfolio is efficient
Proxy (Nifty) is imperfect
Why still use it
Simple, intuitive, and inputs are observable. Treat the answer as a disciplined estimate, not a precise number.

Mention Fama-French as an extension.

SESSION 9 · CAPM COMPONENTS
Risk-free rate and beta
Risk-free rate
Return with no default risk and no reinvestment risk. In India, use the yield on the 10-year Government of India security, matched to the cash-flow horizon and currency.
Market premium
Rm − Rf is the extra return investors demand for equities. Estimated from long-run history or surveys; 5 to 7% is common for India.
Beta
β = Cov(Ri, Rm) / Var(Rm), the slope when stock returns are regressed on market returns. Portfolio beta = weighted average of stock betas.
β
Meaning
Sector examples
< 0.8
Defensive, moves less than market
FMCG, utilities
0.8–1.2
Market-like
Large diversified firms
> 1.2
Aggressive, amplified moves
Autos, metals, real estate
Beta rises with operating leverage and financial leverage; estimates depend on index, period and frequency.

Levered beta = unlevered beta × [1 + (1−t)D/E].

SESSION 9 · STOCK ALPHA
Alpha: return earned beyond what risk explains
Jensen's alpha
α = Rp − [Rf + β(Rm − Rf)]
Actual return minus the CAPM expected return. Regression form: Rp − Rf = α + β(Rm − Rf) + ε.
Interpreting
α > 0: beat its risk-adjusted benchmark (above the SML).
α = 0: fair return.
α < 0: lagged what its risk required.
Applications
How alpha is used
Fund selection
Rank managers by alpha, not raw return
Stock picking
Positive alpha: candidate to buy; negative: sell
Performance fees
Pay for skill, not for taking more beta
Cautions
Alpha depends on the chosen benchmark and period, can be luck (check t-statistic), and should be measured after fees.
21
A fund beating the index may just have a high beta.

SESSION 10 · COUPON AND YIELD TO MATURITY
Three different 'rates' on one bond
Coupon rate
Coupon ÷ face value. Fixed when issued. ₹80 on ₹1,000 = 8%.
Current yield
Annual coupon ÷ market price. ₹80 ÷ ₹974.56 = 8.21%. Ignores the pull to par.
Yield to maturity
The IRR of all promised cash flows: P = Σ C/(1+y)^t + F/(1+y)^n.
Step
Bond: 8% coupon, 3 years, price ₹974.56
Try 8%
PV = ₹1,000 (too high)
Try 9%
80/1.09 + 80/1.188 + 1,080/1.295 = ₹974.56 ✓
YTM
9.0%: coupon 80 + gain 25.44 held to par
Conventions
Semi-annual bond with 3.5% per half-year: YTM (bond-equivalent) = 7.00%; effective annual yield = 1.035² − 1 = 7.12%. YTM assumes no default and coupons reinvested at YTM.

BONDS AND INTEREST RATES · REAL-LIFE EXAMPLES
Hikes hurt prices; cuts lift them
RBI hiking cycle, 2022–23
Repo rate rose from 4.00% to 6.50% (May 2022 to Feb 2023), pushing yields up. A ₹10 lakh face holding of a 10-year 7% bond bought at par falls to ≈ ₹9.33L if the yield rises 1 point: a ₹67,000 mark-to-market loss, partly offset by ₹70,000 yearly coupons.
RBI easing, 2020
Repo cut from 5.15% to 4.00% (Feb to May 2020) as COVID hit. The same bond rises to ≈ ₹10.74L when yield drops 1 point: a ₹74,000 gain, but coupons must now be reinvested at lower rates.
Lesson
Detail
Rates up
Prices down, new bonds pay more
Rates down
Prices up, reinvestment gets cheaper to regret
Long vs short
Long bonds move more; match maturity to your horizon
Why it matters
Banks and funds holding long bonds face losses when rates jump (e.g. Silicon Valley Bank, 2023).

Hold to maturity and the price loss disappears, but the opportunity cost remains.

BLACK-SCHOLES MODEL · OVERVIEW
What it is, what it assumes, why it matters
What it does
Gives the fair value of a European option from five observable inputs. Rests on a replicating portfolio of stock and cash that removes risk (no-arbitrage).
Assumptions
Stock follows lognormal geometric Brownian motion. Constant volatility and rate. No dividends (or a known yield). European exercise. Frictionless, continuous trading and borrowing at the risk-free rate.
Why it is widely used

Instant answer; no simulation needed
Few inputs
Only volatility is unobserved

Market quotes options in σ terms
Hedging
Delta and other Greeks come built in
Common language
Benchmark for traders, regulators, accountants (ESOPs)

SESSION 15 · FCF AND DCF
Free cash flow, DCF and the FCFF versus FCFE choice
FCFF = EBIT(1−t) + D&A − Capex − ΔWC
Discount at WACC → enterprise value; subtract net debt → equity. Reliance: FCFF ₹42,500 cr, WACC 11.7% → ₹1,019 per share.
FCFE = NI + D&A − Capex − ΔWC + net borrowing
Discount at cost of equity → equity value. HDFC Bank: ₹52,000 cr, Ke 12.4% → ₹2,029 per share.
DCF = Σ FCFt/(1+r)^t + TV/(1+r)^n
Reliable for steady businesses (Coca-Cola, WACC 7–8%, g 2–3%); fragile when value rests on far growth. Extra: FCF ₹100, g 3%, r 10% → TV = 103/0.07 ≈ ₹1,471 at year n, then discount it.
Use FCFF when leverage changes; FCFE when debt is the business (banks, NBFCs). Consistent inputs give the same equity value.

SECURITISATION · REAL-LIFE EXAMPLES
Two businesses that fund themselves by selling loan pools
Example 1 · Ford Credit (US auto loans)
What it does: Ford Credit lends to car buyers, pools the retail instalment contracts and sells them to a trust that issues auto asset-backed securities (ABS).
Why: raises cash for new loans without waiting five years for repayment, and widens its funding beyond bank lines and bonds.
Illustrative deal: $1,000m pool, 7% average rate. About 92% senior notes rated AAA. Protection comes from subordinated notes, overcollateralisation and a reserve account.
Result: car loans are short and granular, so losses are predictable. Auto ABS held up far better than subprime mortgage deals in 2008–09.
Example 2 · Indian NBFC (e.g. Shriram Finance)
What it does: an NBFC lending against trucks sells pools of loans to banks and trusts as pass-through certificates (PTCs) or by direct assignment.
Why: converts a 3-year loan book into cash today and eases the gap between short borrowing and long lending.
Illustrative deal: ₹1,000 cr pool at 13%. Senior PTC ₹900 cr rated AAA(SO). First-loss cover of 10% from cash collateral and excess spread.
Rule: RBI's 2021 securitisation directions make the originator keep a minimum stake and hold loans for a minimum period before sale.

SESSION 16 · MORTGAGE-BACKED SECURITIES
MBS: pooling home loans into bonds
How a pass-through MBS pays
Borrowers pay EMI each month. A servicer collects, keeps a fee and passes the rest to MBS holders in proportion.
Illustrative: $100m pool, 30 years, 6% mortgages, 5.5% pass-through. Level payment $599,550 a month.
Month 1: interest $500,000 + principal $99,550. Investors get $458,333 interest + principal. Servicer and guarantee fee: $41,667.
Prepayment risk
Rates fall: homeowners refinance, principal returns early at par, and the investor reinvests at lower yields (contraction).
Rates rise: prepayments slow, the MBS lengthens and its price falls more (extension).
Negative convexity: price gains are capped when yields fall; losses are not when they rise.

MBS · REAL-LIFE EXAMPLES
One safe, one broken: agency MBS versus subprime MBS
Example 1 · Agency MBS (Fannie Mae)
What it does: lenders sell mortgages to Fannie Mae, which pools them, guarantees principal and interest, and sells MBS. Lenders get cash to make new loans. This is why the 30-year fixed US mortgage exists.
Scale: the agency MBS market is worth several trillion dollars, among the largest bond markets.
2022–23: US 30-year mortgage rates rose from about 3% to above 7%. Prepayments slowed, MBS lengthened and prices fell. Banks holding them, such as Silicon Valley Bank, booked large unrealised losses.
Example 2 · Subprime private-label MBS (2006–08)
What happened: lenders such as Countrywide made subprime and low-documentation loans. Banks such as Lehman Brothers and Bear Stearns pooled them into MBS and CDOs, and agencies rated senior tranches AAA.
The break: US house prices fell about 27% from the 2006 peak to 2012. Defaults wiped out mezzanine tranches and hit AAA ones too.
Fallout: two Bear Stearns MBS-heavy hedge funds failed in 2007. Lehman filed for bankruptcy on 15 Sep 2008.
Compare
Agency MBS carry interest-rate and prepayment risk. Private-label MBS add credit and model risk: the models assumed house prices would not fall nationwide.

SESSION 16 · CREDIT DEFAULT SWAPS
CDS: insurance-like protection against a borrower's default
How a CDS works
The protection buyer pays a fixed annual spread on a notional. The protection seller pays the loss if a credit event occurs: bankruptcy, failure to pay or restructuring.
Loss paid = notional × (1 − recovery). Documented under ISDA. The buyer need not own the bond.
Numbers (₹ 100 cr notional)
Premium: 200 bp a year = ₹2 cr.
Default, 40% recovery: seller pays ₹60 cr.
Spread widens to 600 bp: new cover costs ₹6 cr a year, so the old contract gains value.
Implied default: spread ÷ (1 − recovery). 400 bp ÷ 50% = 8%.

CDS · REAL-LIFE EXAMPLES
Two businesses where CDS mattered: AIG and Credit Suisse
Example 1 · AIG (protection seller, 2008)
What it did: AIG Financial Products sold CDS protection on multi-sector CDOs backed by subprime mortgages, about $78bn of exposure. It collected small premiums and held little capital against the risk.
The break: when ratings fell, counterparties demanded collateral. After AIG was downgraded in Sep 2008 it could not pay.
Result: the US Federal Reserve lent $85bn on 16 Sep 2008. Total US support later reached about $182bn.
Lesson: a CDS is only as good as the seller, and risks that fail together cannot be insured by one firm.
Example 2 · Credit Suisse (CDS buyers, 2023)
What happened: after Silicon Valley Bank failed, Credit Suisse's five-year CDS rose from under 400 bp in early March 2023 to about 1,000 bp by mid-March.
Cost: $100m of cover went from about $4m to about $10m a year. With 40% recovery, 1,000 bp implies roughly 16.7% default odds.
Outcome: UBS agreed to buy the bank on 19 Mar 2023. Senior creditors were paid in full, but about CHF 16bn of AT1 bonds were written to zero.
Lesson: the CDS spread flagged stress before any default.
Compare
AIG shows the seller's risk: collateral calls and concentration. Credit Suisse shows the buyer's signal: the price of protection as a stress gauge. Figures are rounded.
