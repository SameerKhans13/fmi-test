Assets: What the Business Owns
An asset is an economic resource the business owns or controls that is expected to give future economic benefit.
Current assets
Realised, sold or consumed within the operating cycle or about 12 months: cash, bank balance, receivables, inventory, short-term investments, prepaid expenses.
Non-current assets
Benefit over a longer period: buildings, machinery, computers, vehicles, long-term investments, certain intangibles, right-of-use (lease) assets.
Kirana store: cash ₹50,000, stock ₹3L and customers owing ₹20,000 are current. The ₹1.2L freezer and shop fittings are non-current.
4
Order of liquidity: cash first, then receivables, inventory, then long-term items. Receivables are what customers owe; prepaid expenses are services paid for in advance, such as a year of insurance.
4

Liabilities & Equity: Who funds the assets
Current liabilities
Due within the operating cycle or about 12 months: accounts payable, salaries payable, interest payable, taxes payable, short-term borrowings.
Non-current liabilities
Due beyond 12 months: long-term bank loans, long-term bonds, certain lease liabilities.
A liability is an obligation to an outsider. Equity is the owners' residual claim: Equity = Assets − Liabilities, from capital invested and profits retained, reduced by losses and drawings or dividends.
Store example: supplier bill ₹80,000 and salary due ₹30,000 are current; a ₹5L five-year bank loan is non-current.
5
The classification matters for liquidity ratios: only current items count in the current ratio. A long-term loan's instalment due within 12 months moves into current liabilities.
5

Accounting Equation
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
Proprietorship
Equity = Owner's capital + Profit − Drawings
Company
Equity = Share capital + Retained earnings + Other reserves
Seven elements: assets, liabilities, equity, income, expenses, gains and losses. Income, expenses, gains and losses change equity: income and gains raise it, expenses and losses reduce it, so Equity moves by Income − Expenses + Gains − Losses (before drawings or dividends).
The equation must balance after every transaction. Every ₹ of assets is funded by outsiders or by owners.
6
A gain arises from incidental events such as selling an old asset above its carrying amount; a loss when sold below. Income and expenses come from regular operations.

Four conventions that guide judgment
Consistency
Keep the same method period to period. Changing depreciation yearly to shape profit destroys comparability.
Prudence
Be cautious under uncertainty: do not overstate assets or income.
Full disclosure
Give users the material information needed to understand position and performance.
Materiality
Information matters if omitting or misstating it could influence users' decisions.
13
A ₹500 stationery misstatement is immaterial for a large company but a ₹5L error in receivables would likely be material.

Three Important Financial Statements
Income Statement
Performance over a period. Revenue − expenses = profit.
Balance Sheet
Position on one date. Assets = Liabilities + Equity.
Cash Flow
Cash in and out: operating, investing, financing.
How they link: profit flows into equity on the balance sheet. Depreciation lowers profit but is not a cash outflow.
Depreciation example: machine ₹10L, residual ₹1L, life 5 years → (10 − 1) ÷ 5 = ₹1.8L expense each year (straight line).
17
Profit is not cash: a business can be profitable and still run out of cash if receivables pile up. Matching principle spreads asset cost over its useful life.
17

Depreciation: Spreading an asset's cost over its life
Depreciation allocates the cost of a non-current asset over the years it helps earn revenue (matching concept). Four terms:
Cost
What the machine cost: ₹11,00,000
Residual value
Expected disposal value: ₹1,00,000
Useful life
Years of use: 5
Depreciable amount
Cost − residual = ₹10,00,000
Two methods follow: straight line (same charge every year) and reducing balance (a fixed percentage of the opening book value). Example for a laptop: cost ₹60,000, residual ₹10,000, 5 years → ₹10,000 a year on straight line.
18
Depreciation is a non-cash expense: it lowers profit but no cash leaves in the year. The laptop example is an added illustration.

Intangible Assets
Identifiable non-monetary assets with no physical substance that the business controls and expects to give future benefit.
Examples
Patents, copyrights, trademarks, brands, software licences, franchises, customer relationships, technology, qualifying R&D.
Recognise only if
Future benefits are probable and cost can be measured reliably. Externally bought: at cost.

Cash flow Statement: Where cash came from and went
It shows inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents in a period. The income statement shows profit; this statement explains the change in cash.
Operating
Day-to-day business. In: cash from customers and services. Out: suppliers, salaries, rent and other expenses, taxes.
Investing
Long-term assets. Out: buying machinery, buildings, long-term investments. In: selling them.
Financing
Capital. In: shares, debentures, bank loans. Out: buybacks, dividends, interest, loan repayment.

Operating cash flow is the health check: a sustainable business funds itself from operations. Interest on debt is shown here under financing, as in the source.

Financial Ratios: Current Ratio
Current ratio
Current assets ÷ current liabilities. Rule of thumb 1.5–2. Example: ₹60L ÷ ₹40L = 1.5.
Quick ratio
(Current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities. (60 − 20) ÷ 40 = 1.0.
Cash ratio
Cash and equivalents ÷ current liabilities. ₹8L ÷ ₹40L = 0.2.
Debt-to-equity
Total debt ÷ equity. ₹50L ÷ ₹100L = 0.5. Higher means more risk.
Interest coverage
EBIT ÷ interest: ₹24L ÷ ₹6L = 4×. Below 2 is a warning sign.
Five families: liquidity, efficiency, leverage, profitability, market.
30
Ratios only mean something against past years, peers and budget. Inventory is excluded from the quick ratio because it can be slow to turn into cash.
30

Efficiency Ratios: How fast does money cycle?
Inventory days
Days stock sits before sale. COGS ₹140L ÷ stock ₹20L = 7×, so 365 ÷ 7 = 52 days.
Receivable days
Days customers take to pay. Sales ₹200L ÷ debtors ₹25L = 8×, so 46 days.
Payable days
Days we take to pay suppliers. COGS ₹140L ÷ creditors ₹28L = 5×, so 73 days.
Operating cycle = Inventory days + Receivable days − Payable days
Example: 45 + 30 − 35 = 40 days of cash tied up. Shorten it by collecting faster or stretching suppliers within terms. Solvency ratios (D/E, interest cover) cover long-term obligations.
31
Turnover ratios: COGS ÷ average inventory; credit sales ÷ average receivables; credit purchases (or COGS) ÷ average payables. Days = 365 ÷ turnover.
31

Profitability Ratios: How well do we earn?
Gross margin
(Sales − COGS) ÷ Sales: (200 − 140) ÷ 200 = 30%
Operating margin
EBIT ÷ Sales: 24 ÷ 200 = 12%
Net margin
PAT ÷ Sales: 14 ÷ 200 = 7%
ROCE
EBIT ÷ capital employed: 24 ÷ 150 = 16%
ROE
PAT ÷ equity: 14 ÷ 100 = 14%
Asset turnover
Sales ÷ total assets: 200 ÷ 190 = 1.05×
DuPont: ROE = Net margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier. Retail wins on turnover, luxury on margin.
32
DuPont shows why two firms with the same ROE can be very different businesses. High ROE from heavy leverage is risky.

Absorbing Overheads into Product Cost
Total production cost = Direct material + Direct labour + Production overheads. Unit cost is needed to value stock, set prices, measure profit, compare products and decide to make or outsource.
The question
A motor uses ₹4,000 direct material and ₹2,000 direct labour (₹6,000 direct). Factory rent, power, depreciation, supervisor pay, maintenance and security also exist. How much belongs to each motor?
Absorption
Spread overheads over units using an absorption rate: OAR = Total overhead ÷ activity base (units, labour hours or machine hours).
Source example: overhead ₹1,00,000 ÷ 20,000 units = ₹5 absorbed into every unit.
51
The deck's example used dollars; rupees are used here. A base should reflect what drives the overhead: labour hours for labour-intensive work, machine hours for automated plants.

Cost Volume Profit (CVP) Analysis
Questions
How many units to break even? To earn a target profit? What if volume, price, variable cost or fixed cost changes? How far can sales fall before a loss?
Key terms
Selling price (SP), variable cost (VC), contribution = SP − VC, fixed cost (FC), break-even point (BEP).
At break-even, total revenue = total cost, so profit is zero. Contribution covers fixed costs first, then adds to profit. Assumes costs split cleanly into fixed and variable, and constant price and unit variable cost within the relevant range.

Break-even can be stated in units or in sales value. The next slides use two businesses: a shoe manufacturer and a cloud kitchen.
